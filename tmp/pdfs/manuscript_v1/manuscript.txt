Abstract


Semantic caching improves LLM system efficiency by storing and reusing responses
for semantically similar queries. Most existing frameworks rely on a single embedding
model, which struggles to cover the different types of semantic relationships found in
real query traffic. We build a stacking ensemble that combines a bi-encoder, a cross-
encoder, and a set of geometric and text-based features through a LightGBM meta-
classifier. We evaluate the approach on the Quora Question Pairs (QQP) dataset,
validate it on the Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) benchmark, and use QQP query
pairs to measure per-component latency and feature-group contributions. A feature-
level ablation shows that most of this gain comes from geometric features derived
from the bi-encoder embeddings rather than from the cross-encoder itself, whose re-
moval changes the ensemble F1 score by less than 0.001 despite accounting for over
95% of the added inference latency. The cross-encoder can therefore be treated as

1. Introduction

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has transformed natural
language processing applications, with models from OpenAI, Meta, and Anthropic
demonstrating remarkable capabilities in text summarization, translation, content
generation, and sentiment analysis (Brown et al., 2020; Jin et al., 2025; Guo et al.,
2026). However, the computational expense of LLM inference, coupled with token-
based pricing models, creates significant cost and latency challenges for production
systems (Huang et al., 2024).

Research indicates that approximately 31% of ChatGPT queries exhibit semantic
similarity to previously submitted requests (Gill et al., 2024), revealing substantial
inefficiencies in current LLM deployment strategies. Since users incur costs based
on both input and output tokens processed, optimizing the handling of repetitive
queries presents a critical opportunity to reduce operational expenses and improve
system responsiveness.

Semantic caching addresses this challenge by storing and reusing responses based on


                                          2
the meaning of queries rather than exact textual matches (Luo et al., 2025). Unlike
traditional keyword-based caching, semantic caching leverages embedding models
(Reimers and Gurevych, 2019; Deerwester et al., 1990; Tu et al., 2024) to identify
semantically equivalent queries, enabling response reuse across paraphrased or refor-
mulated requests. This approach has been implemented in systems like GPTCache
(Bang et al., 2023), an open-source framework that provides modular components
for embedding generation, similarity evaluation, and cache management.

Despite these advances, current semantic caching systems face a fundamental limi-
tation: their reliance on single-sentence transformer models for semantic comparison
(Li et al., 2025b). While individual models excel in specific semantic dimensions, for
example, bi-encoders provide efficient similarity scoring but may miss nuanced se-
mantic interactions that cross-encoders capture through token-level attention. Thus,
no single model consistently performs well across all query types and domains.

To address these limitations, we contribute two aspects that target both seman-
tic similarity detection and cache management optimization. First, we develop a
stacking ensemble method for improved query similarity assessment that combines
complementary embedding architectures: a bi-encoder for efficient initial similarity
scoring and a cross-encoder for capturing fine-grained semantic interactions. Then,
a LightGBM serves as a meta-classifier to optimally weight and combine the simi-
larity predictions. Second, we introduce a novel Semantic Decay eviction policy for
intelligent cache replacement that incorporates both temporal access patterns and
semantic relationships between cached queries, moving beyond traditional frequency
and recency-based approaches to preserve semantically valuable cache entries.

We evaluate our approach comprehensively using the Quora Question Pairs (QQP)


                                          3
dataset (Iyer et al., 2017) as our primary benchmark and validate cross-domain
adaptability on the Semantic Text Similarity (STS) benchmark dataset (Cer et al.,
2017). Our evaluation framework measures cache hit ratios, token savings, and
response time improvements to assess caching effectiveness. Our evaluation demon-
strates that the stacking ensemble approach achieves substantially improved cache
performance metrics with significant relative improvements over both individual
models and traditional eviction policies.

While earlier work has combined bi-encoders and cross-encoders for semantic simi-
larity, most of these combinations are evaluated purely on classification metrics and
rarely measure the actual computational price of adding a cross-encoder to a caching
pipeline that must respond in real time. A caching system is only useful if it is fast
enough to beat calling the LLM directly, so accuracy gains that come at a large la-
tency cost need to be reported together with that cost, not separately. We take this
position seriously in our evaluation: alongside classification metrics, we measure the
per-query latency contributed by each component of our stacking ensemble on the
same QQP query pairs used for our accuracy evaluation, so that the latency-accuracy
tradeoff is reported on a single, consistent dataset rather than inferred indirectly.

The primary contributions of this work are:

  1. a stacking ensemble framework that combines bi-encoder embeddings, cross-
     encoder scores, and a set of geometric and text-based features through a trained
     meta-classifier, giving a substantial improvement in similarity detection over
     any single model,
  2. a systematic comparison of meta-classifier choices, including LightGBM, XG-
     Boost, and Random Forest, across multiple feature configurations and two


                                            4
     datasets,
  3. a feature-level ablation study on QQP query pairs, showing that most of the
     ensemble’s accuracy gain comes from geometric features computed directly
     from the bi-encoder embeddings, while the cross-encoder contributes only a
     small, sometimes negligible improvement despite accounting for most of the
     added latency, which gives practitioners a concrete basis for deciding whether
     the cross-encoder is worth including in a given deployment,
  4. a latency and feature-importance analysis on QQP query pairs, measured on
     the same dataset and labeling scheme used for our accuracy evaluation, giving
     a consistent basis for reasoning about the latency-accuracy tradeoff,
  5. cross-dataset validation demonstrating generalizability across QQP binary clas-
     sification and STS regression tasks.


2. Related Work

In contemporary engineering applications, the optimization of machine learning method-
ologies and deep neural networks has emerged as a critical focus area (Guo et al.,
2025; Naderi and Shojaei, 2025; Gebreab et al., 2026; Cremaschi et al., 2025). The
trends of model compression, alongside the minimization of processing, inference,
and search times, are increasingly pertinent. This paper specifically addresses the
imperative of reducing processing times associated with query processing and the
efficient retrieval of relevant responses within Large Language Models (LLMs). By
streamlining these aspects, we aim to enhance the overall performance and applica-
bility of such advanced systems.




                                            5
2.1. Semantic Caching for LLMs
The challenge of optimizing LLM inference costs has led to significant research in
semantic caching approaches. Early frameworks like GPTCache established the foun-
dation for semantic similarity-based response reuse, utilizing single embedding mod-
els to identify semantically equivalent queries. However, recent work has identified
several limitations in these baseline approaches, leading to more sophisticated caching
strategies (Tu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024).

Semantic Caching for Automated LLMs (SCALM) addresses inefficiencies in tradi-
tional semantic caching by incorporating pattern detection and frequency analysis
(Li et al., 2024). The system identifies the most frequent cache entries and query
patterns in LLM-based chat services, ranking queries based on detected patterns
to optimize cache hit ratios. Experimental evaluation demonstrates that SCALM
achieves a 63% relative increase in cache hit ratio and a 77% reduction in token
usage compared to GPTCache. Despite these improvements, SCALM maintains the
single embedding model paradigm, which limits its ability to generalize across diverse
query semantics and complex real-world interactions.

Generative Caching for LLMs introduces a dynamic approach that balances cost,
latency, and response quality through adaptive similarity thresholds (Iyengar et al.,
2025). The system adjusts thresholds based on query complexity, user preferences,
and computational constraints. For instance, code generation queries receive higher
similarity thresholds to ensure response accuracy, while simple question-answering
tasks operate with lower thresholds to maximize cache utilization. The framework
additionally supports response synthesis for novel queries by combining cached re-
sponses. Performance evaluation shows nine-fold speed improvements over GPT-
Cache, with customizable parameters allowing users to select domain-appropriate

                                           6
embedding models.

2.1.1. Evolution Beyond Static Thresholds
Recent research has identified critical limitations in first-generation semantic caching
systems that rely on static similarity thresholds. VectorQ addresses this brittleness
by introducing adaptive thresholding mechanisms that dynamically adjust decision
boundaries based on query characteristics and embedding distributions (Schroeder
et al., 2025). This demonstrates that significant performance gains can be achieved
not just through better similarity metrics, but through more intelligent decision-
making processes.

2.1.2. Context-Aware Caching Paradigms
The assumption that queries can be evaluated in isolation has proven flawed for
multi-turn conversational applications. ContextCache introduces a two-stage re-
trieval architecture that uses self-attention mechanisms to integrate current queries
with historical dialogue context (Yan et al., 2025). This context-aware approach en-
ables disambiguation of semantically similar but contextually distinct queries, high-
lighting that the definition of a "match" is itself context-dependent.

2.1.3. From Retrieval to Augmentation and Refinement
The most recent advancements represent a conceptual leap, reframing caches from
passive response repositories to active knowledge bases. IC-Cache leverages historical
request-response pairs as in-context examples to augment new prompts, enabling
smaller models to be guided by larger "teacher" models (Yu et al., 2025). Similarly,
TweakLLM proposes a two-tier architecture where cached responses are refined by
small "tweaker" models rather than returned identically (Cheema et al., 2025). These
approaches demonstrate that semantic caching principles can extend beyond final

                                           7
query-response pairs to intermediate workflow artifacts (Couturier et al., 2025).

2.2. Stacking Ensemble Approaches for Semantic Similarity

The motivation for combining bi-encoder and cross-encoder architectures is well-
established in information retrieval literature. Bi-encoders provide computational
efficiency through independent sentence encoding, while cross-encoders offer supe-
rior accuracy through joint processing at a higher computational cost (Reimers and
Gurevych, 2019).

A direct precedent for this combination is the Sentence-CROBI architecture, which
explicitly combines signals from both architectures for semantic textual relatedness
tasks (Ortiz-Barajas and Bel-Enguix, 2024). However, Sentence-CROBI relies on
manual feature engineering, concatenating the cross-encoder’s [CLS] token embed-
ding, bi-encoder Euclidean distances, and raw sentence embeddings into a fixed vec-
tor for a simple MLP.

Our stacking approach represents a methodological advance through the use of
stacked generalization rather than fixed concatenation. The LightGBM meta-classifier
learns optimal, non-linear combinations of diverse similarity signals from training
data, moving beyond engineered fusion to learned fusion strategies. This progression
from cascade approaches (bi-encoder retrieval, cross-encoder re-ranking) to engi-
neered unification (Sentence-CROBI) to learned unification (our stacking ensemble)
represents the evolution of semantic similarity modeling.

2.3. Semantic Caching in the Broader LLM Optimization Context

Semantic caching represents one component of a comprehensive LLM optimization
ecosystem. Complementary techniques target different aspects of inference efficiency:


                                         8
speculative decoding reduces per-token generation costs through draft model accel-
eration (Bhattacharjee et al., 2024), quantization methods like SiLQ reduce memory
footprints through lower-precision representations (Esser et al., 2025), and knowl-
edge distillation techniques like Delta-KD enable smaller student models to capture
larger teacher model capabilities (Cao et al., 2025). Other lines of work target the
architecture of the model itself rather than the serving pipeline around it. Neural
block linearization reduces the cost of attention computation by replacing selected
transformer blocks with linear approximations (Erdogan et al., 2025), while recurrent
architectures such as xLSTM 7B aim to reach comparable quality to transformer-
based LLMs with faster and more memory-efficient inference (Beck et al., 2025).
At the level of decoding strategy rather than the model architecture itself, Min-
ions accelerates inference by having several small speculative models jointly propose
candidate tokens through a majority-voting mechanism, which the main LLM then
verifies, improving on single-speculator approaches (Wang et al., 2024b). These tech-
niques address different bottlenecks than semantic caching, since they change how
a single query is processed, while semantic caching changes whether a query needs
to be processed at all, but they remain relevant to caching system design because a
faster underlying LLM changes the latency budget available for similarity detection
at the front end.

These approaches are synergistic rather than competitive. A production system
might employ distilled and quantized models served via speculative decoding, with
the entire pipeline fronted by semantic caching using our proposed stacking ensem-
ble. This positions our contributions as one component among several in end-to-end
optimization strategies for efficient LLM deployment.

3. Methodology

In this section, we present our comprehensive approach to semantic caching by estab-
lishing (1) a theoretical foundation that grounds our work in established principles,
(2) the overall system architecture that operationalizes these principles, (3) a stack-
ing ensemble design that systematically combines multiple similarity signals, (4) a
feature extraction pipeline capturing semantic relationships at multiple levels, and
(5) a novel Semantic Decay eviction policy that incorporates semantic relationships
into cache management decisions.

3.1. Theoretical Foundation

3.1.1. Semantic Caching Framework
Semantic caching operates within the theoretical framework of approximate similarity
search in high-dimensional spaces, extending traditional caching theory to account


                                              10
for semantic relationships rather than exact matches. Formally, given a query q and
cache entries C = {c1 , c2 , ..., cn }, we seek to determine if there exists ci ∈ C such
that sim(q, ci ) ≥ τ , where τ is a similarity threshold and sim is a semantic similarity
function.

This formulation transforms caching from a simple lookup problem to a hypothesis
testing problem in metric spaces, where we aim to maximize the probability of correct
cache hits while minimizing false positives under semantic similarity constraints.

3.1.2. Ensemble Learning Theory
Our stacking ensemble approach is grounded in the bias-variance decomposition
framework and the theory of stacked generalization. Individual embedding mod-
els exhibit complementary error patterns due to their different architectural biases:

                             simB (q, c) = cos(fB (q), fB (c))                       (1)

                                  simC (q, c) = fC (q, c)                            (2)

The bi-encoder fB provides computational efficiency through independent encoding
but may miss nuanced token-level interactions. The cross-encoder fC captures fine-
grained semantic relationships through joint encoding but at a higher computational
cost. Our stacking ensemble learns the following optimal combination:

                  simensemble (q, c) = g(simB (q, c), simC (q, c), ϕ(q, c))          (3)

where g is a meta-classifier and ϕ represents additional feature transformations. This
approach theoretically achieves superior performance by reducing variance through
model averaging while minimizing bias through learned combination weights.




                                             11
3.2. Semantic Caching System Architecture

As illustrated in Figure 1, our system architecture integrates a dual-embedding
framework with a comprehensive cache management system. The embedding frame-
work consists of two specialized models: a bi-encoder for generating query embed-
dings and a cross-encoder for directly computing similarity scores between query
pairs. These complementary similarity signals are then integrated through a meta-
classifier implemented using LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017), which makes the final
similarity determination. When the highest similarity score exceeds a predefined
threshold, the system retrieves and returns the cached response associated with the
most similar stored query. Conversely, when no sufficiently similar query is found
(cache miss), the LLM generates a new response, which is subsequently stored in
both CacheBase and VectorBase to enhance future query processing.

training methodology.

3.3.2. Meta-Classifier Selection and Training
Meta-Classifier Selection: Extensive evaluation across multiple architectures and
feature configurations reveals that LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) with extended fea-
tures achieves optimal performance (F1: 0.8716 ± 0.0040, AUC: 0.9336 ± 0.0049).
Extended features consistently improve all classifiers, with gradient boosting methods
(LightGBM, XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016)) demonstrating superior perfor-
mance compared to Random Forest (Breiman, 2001), indicating their effectiveness
for this feature combination task.

Training Procedure: The stacking ensemble is trained using a two-stage process
that first extracts features from base models, then trains the meta-classifier on labeled
query pairs from the QQP dataset.

During training, each query pair is processed through both the bi-encoder and cross-
encoder to extract base features. The bi-encoder generates embeddings and computes
cosine similarity, while the cross-encoder produces direct similarity scores. When
extended features are enabled, the system computes supplementary metrics from the
embeddings and text properties.

We employ stratified k-fold cross-validation to rigorously train and evaluate our mod-


                                           14
els. The value of k for each component is judiciously determined based on a com-
prehensive analysis of experimental results, data volume, and hardware constraints.
This methodological approach ensures robust performance assessment and enhances
the reliability of our findings. The following are considered:

   • Bi-encoders: 5-fold cross-validation on 50,000 balanced samples

   • Cross-encoders: 3-fold cross-validation on 10,000 balanced samples

   • Meta-classifier: 5-fold cross-validation on 30,000 balanced samples with fea-
     tures extracted from pre-selected best base models

For each fold, the meta-classifier is trained on the combined feature representations
from the training portion, with hyperparameters optimized through grid search. The
fold-specific optimal threshold (for individual models) or probability threshold (for
the ensemble) is determined on the validation portion. Training configuration varies
by meta-classifier type: the tree-based models, LightGBM and XGBoost, use early
stopping based on validation performance with regularization parameters optimized
through cross-validation, while Random Forest employs out-of-bag error estimation
with hyperparameters tuned via grid search. Final performance metrics are com-
puted as the average across all folds, with standard deviations reported to indicate
consistency.

3.4. Semantic Decay Eviction Policy

Traditional cache eviction policies like LRU and LFU rely solely on temporal or
frequency-based metrics, ignoring the semantic relationships between cached queries.
Our novel semantic decay policy addresses this limitation by incorporating both tem-
poral dynamics and semantic relationships through a comprehensive scoring mecha-


                                          15
nism that determines which entries to evict when the cache reaches capacity.

The policy computes a composite score for each cached query q at time t using the
following formula:




                 Score(q, t) = w1 × F reshness(q, t) + w2 × Age(q, t)
                                                                                    (4)
                               + w3 × Boost(q, t)


Each component plays a distinct role in the eviction decision. Freshness(q, t) quanti-
fies a query’s relevance to recent access patterns by calculating the average semantic
similarity between q and the n most recent queries, retaining entries that align with
contemporary usage. Age(q, t) assesses the temporal stability of a query’s relevance
through an exponential decay function e−λ(t−t0 ) , where t0 is the entry’s creation time
and λ is a learned decay rate, gradually reducing relevance over time while still
preserving entries of historical significance. Boost(q, t) introduces a semantic clus-
tering effect by augmenting the scores of entries semantically similar to frequently
accessed queries, maintaining coherent semantic neighborhoods within the cache and
optimizing retrieval for related queries.

The weights associated with these components were optimized through a grid search
on validation data: w1 = 0.28 for Freshness, prioritizing relevance to the present
workload; w2 = 0.38 for Age, emphasizing temporal stability for sustained utility;
and w3 = 0.34 for Boost, sustaining semantic clustering among contextually related
queries. When the cache reaches its maximum capacity, entries with the lowest com-
posite scores are evicted first, so the cache retains not only frequently or recently
accessed entries but also those with the highest semantic value to the current and an-


                                            16
ticipated workload. The policy adapts to changing query patterns while maintaining
semantic coherence, yielding higher hit ratios than conventional caching methodolo-
gies.

3.5. Evaluation Framework

3.5.1. LLM and System Configuration
In our evaluation of response generation in caching mechanisms, we employ the
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 model, utilizing a system prompt that directs it to act as "a
helpful assistant who responds briefly." This systematic configuration is strategically
designed to ensure the conciseness of the generated responses, thereby facilitating
the efficient caching of outputs for rejected queries while minimizing latency.

3.5.2. Evaluation Metrics
The assessment of a caching system, particularly one grounded in semantic similarity,
necessitates the application of several pivotal metrics that elucidate its effectiveness
and efficiency. Our study concentrates on three primary metrics: hit ratio, token
savings, and response time. For our stacking ensemble model, these metrics are de-
rived from the learned decision boundaries of the meta-classifier (utilizing a standard
probability threshold of 0.5), as opposed to explicitly defined similarity thresholds.
This approach allows the ensemble to learn optimal feature combination weights that
inherently capture the appropriate decision boundaries.

Cache Hit Ratio: The cache hit ratio quantifies the proportion of requests that
successfully align with semantically similar queries stored within the cache. By
retrieving relevant cached responses, this metric significantly mitigates redundant
computational efforts. The complementary metric, the miss ratio, identifies instances
where queries are correctly categorized as dissimilar, thereby indicating that they are

3.5.3. Similarity Threshold Optimization
We employ systematic threshold optimization (0.0-1.0 range, 101 values) with F1-
score maximization as the objective. Each validation fold determines its optimal
threshold independently using 5-fold cross-validation for bi-encoders and 3-fold for
cross-encoders. For the stacking ensemble, we utilize probability thresholds with the
meta-classifier learning optimal decision boundaries.

Implementation Details: The threshold optimization iterates through all can-
didate thresholds for each validation fold, computing F1-scores and selecting the
threshold that maximizes performance for that specific fold. Each fold’s results are
then computed using its respective optimal threshold, and final metrics represent the
average performance across all folds using their individually optimized thresholds.


                                         18
Stacking Ensemble Threshold Handling: For the stacking ensemble, we utilize
a different approach. The meta-classifier (LightGBM) outputs probabilities, with
a standard 0.5 threshold for binary decisions in training and evaluation. Rather
than optimizing explicit similarity thresholds, the ensemble learns optimal feature
combination weights that inherently capture the appropriate decision boundary. For
semantic caching applications, this 0.5 probability threshold can be adjusted based
on system requirements, with higher thresholds for conservative caching and lower
thresholds for aggressive caching. This dual approach ensures fair comparison be-
tween individual models, with optimized thresholds, and the ensemble approach, with
learned decision boundaries, providing realistic performance estimates for production
deployment scenarios.

3.5.4. Similarity Search Configuration
We employ cosine similarity as our distance metric and utilize FAISS (Douze et al.,
2024) for efficient similarity search. The Flat index method is selected to ensure
exact nearest neighbor retrieval based on cosine similarity, trading computational
cost for accuracy in similarity-based cache performance (Robertson et al., 2009; Mai
et al., 2024).

The semantic caching system integrates three components: CacheBase, a Redis-based
store for query-response pairs with configurable persistence and retrieval operations;
VectorBase, a FAISS-powered vector store with support for both CPU and GPU
acceleration that maintains query-to-ID mappings for efficient operations; and Evic-
tionPolicy, a configurable cache management module supporting LRU, LFU, and
Semantic Decay strategies with policy-specific optimization parameters.

3.6.1. System Properties and Complexity Analysis
The semantic caching system exhibits the following key properties:

   • Computational Complexity:

        – Bi-encoder encoding: O(d) per query

        – Nearest neighbor search: O(|C|·d) using linear scan, improvable to O(log |C|·
          d) with optimized indices

        – Cross-encoder scoring: O(L2 · d) where L is sequence length


                                             22
        – Meta-classifier inference: O(M ) where M is number of features

   • Memory Requirements:

        – VectorBase: O(|C| · d) for storing embeddings

        – CacheBase: O(|C| · Lavg ) for storing query-response pairs

        – Model parameters: O(PB + PC + PM ) for bi-encoder, cross-encoder, and
           meta-classifier

This mathematical formulation provides a rigorous foundation for the semantic caching
process illustrated in Figure 1 and enables precise analysis of system behavior and
performance characteristics.

Cache Size Selection and Scaling Considerations. The cache sizes used in our eviction
policy comparison (300, 500, and 800 entries) and in our QQP caching evaluation
(1500 and 2000 entries) were chosen to keep experiments computationally manageable
while still covering a range large enough to show how eviction behavior changes as the
cache fills up. These sizes are small compared to production caches, which may hold
tens of thousands of entries or more, and we do not evaluate memory consumption
or retrieval latency at that scale in this work.


4. Experimental Setup

This section describes the experimental setup used to evaluate our proposed stacking
ensemble approach. We begin by detailing the datasets and preprocessing procedures,
followed by the base model selection methodology. The experimental design ensures
fair comparison and rigorous validation of our method in multiple dimensions, includ-
ing classification performance, caching efficiency, and cross-domain generalizability,

                                          23
following established practices in semantic similarity evaluation (Nguyen et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2024a).

4.1. Datasets and Preprocessing

4.1.1. Quora Question Pairs (QQP) Dataset
We selected the Quora Question Pairs (QQP) dataset for our primary evaluation
due to its direct alignment with semantic caching requirements and real-world query
characteristics. The dataset consists of over 400,000 question pairs collected from
the Quora platform, where each pair contains two independently formulated ques-
tions submitted by different users across diverse topics, including technology, science,
lifestyle, and general knowledge.

Each question pair in QQP is annotated with a binary label indicating semantic
equivalence: label 1 denotes semantically equivalent questions (duplicates that should
result in cache hits), while label 0 indicates non-equivalent questions (unique queries
requiring new LLM inference).

Stratified Sampling Protocol: We split the original dataset by class labels, du-
plicate versus non-duplicate, and sample equal numbers from each class to create
balanced subsets, eliminating potential bias from class imbalance and following best
practices in machine learning evaluation (Smith et al., 2025). Subset sizes are chosen
according to the computational requirements of each model architecture: bi-encoder
evaluation uses 50,000 samples (25,000 positive, 25,000 negative); cross-encoder eval-
uation uses 10,000 samples (5,000 positive, 5,000 negative), reflecting its higher com-
putational demands; and stacking ensemble training uses 30,000 samples (15,000
positive, 15,000 negative).



                                          24
4.1.2. STS Benchmark Validation Dataset
To validate the generalizability of our stacking ensemble approach across different
semantic similarity paradigms and linguistic domains, we conduct a comprehensive
evaluation on the Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) benchmark dataset Cer et al.
(2017). This dataset provides a standardized evaluation framework for semantic sim-
ilarity methods, and enables assessment of our approach beyond question-answering
domains, following the evaluation methodology used in recent semantic similarity
research (Chen et al., 2025).

Dataset Structure and Coverage: The STS benchmark dataset (Cer et al., 2017)
contains 8,628 sentence pairs distributed across train (5,749), development (1,500),
and test (1,379) splits.

Evaluation Formulations: The continuous nature of STS annotations (Cer et al.,
2017) enables two complementary evaluation approaches that validate different as-
pects of our ensemble method. The regression formulation directly predicts continu-
ous similarity scores (0–5 range) using correlation-based metrics (Pearson, Spearman)
and error-based metrics (RMSE, MAE), validating the ensemble’s ability to provide
confidence-weighted similarity scoring for advanced caching strategies and nuanced
semantic assessment, consistent with established STS evaluation practices (Nguyen
et al., 2025). The classification formulation instead converts continuous scores to
binary labels using a threshold of 2.5, corresponding to "moderately similar," di-
rectly paralleling the QQP evaluation methodology and validating consistent binary
decision-making performance across different datasets and domains. Together, these
two formulations enable comprehensive validation of our stacking ensemble across
both the binary cache hit/miss decisions required for semantic caching systems and



                                         25
the continuous similarity scoring capabilities that demonstrate broader applicability
to semantic similarity tasks (Wang et al., 2024a).

Model Pool Filtering for STS Evaluation: To ensure unbiased evaluation and
prevent data leakage, we filtered our initial model pool to exclude models that were
pre-trained on the STS benchmark dataset. Specifically, we removed models contain-
ing "stsb" in their names (e.g., cross-encoder/stsb-roberta-large, cross-encoder/stsb-
roberta-base) from the STS evaluation pool, as these models have been explicitly
trained on STS data and would provide artificially inflated performance metrics. This
filtering ensures that our cross-dataset validation represents genuine generalization
capability rather than memorization of training data, following rigorous evaluation
standards (Smith et al., 2025).

4.1.3. QQP Subset for Latency and Ablation Analysis
In addition to the stratified, class-balanced QQP subsets used for base-model and
meta-classifier training (Section 4.1), we draw a held-out sample of 3,000 QQP pairs
for the per-component latency breakdown (Section 5.3.3) and the feature-group
ablation study (Section 5.4), disjoint from the training and evaluation folds used
elsewhere in this paper. Unlike the balanced subsets used for model training, this
sample follows a 60/40 duplicate-to-non-duplicate ratio (1,800 duplicate, 1,200 non-
duplicate) to better reflect the higher proportion of semantically repeated queries
observed in production LLM traffic (Gill et al., 2024), rather than the artificially
balanced distribution used to eliminate training bias. We report F1 rather than
accuracy as our primary ablation metric specifically because F1 is more robust to
this class imbalance, and we additionally verify that the feature-group rankings in
Table 6 are stable under a balanced 1,500/1,500 resampling of the same pool (F1
changes by less than 0.01 for every feature group under resampling).

6. Limitations and Bottlenecks

While our stacking ensemble approach demonstrates significant improvements in se-
mantic caching performance, it is essential to critically examine its limitations and
practical constraints. This section discusses computational overhead, model depen-
dencies, and maintenance requirements that impact real-world deployment.

6.1. Computational Overhead

Our stacking ensemble approach introduces additional computational complexity
compared to single-model approaches. The ensemble requires dual model inference,
with both bi-encoder embedding generation and cross-encoder pairwise scoring com-
puted for each query comparison, effectively doubling the base computational cost.
Additionally, the LightGBM meta-classifier adds an inference step, though this over-
head is minimal compared to transformer-based models.

Extended feature extraction introduces preprocessing overhead that scales with query
complexity. Distance metrics computation (||e1 − e2 ||2 , ||e1 − e2 ||1 ), element-wise


                                          39
operations (e1 ⊙ e2 , |e1 − e2 |), and text-based features require additional computation
cycles that may become prohibitive in latency-critical applications.

6.1.1. Memory Requirements
The ensemble approach requires maintaining multiple model weights simultaneously,
with both bi-encoder and cross-encoder models loaded in memory, approximately
doubling the memory footprint compared to single-model approaches. Extended
feature computation requires additional memory for intermediate calculations and
feature vectors, further increasing resource requirements.

6.2. Model Dependency and Maintenance
6.2.1. Base Model Limitations
Our approach inherits limitations from underlying models. Bi-encoder embedding
models may fail to capture nuanced semantic differences in domain-specific contexts
or subtle query variations that humans would easily distinguish. Cross-encoders,
while more accurate, are computationally expensive and may become bottlenecks in
high-throughput scenarios requiring thousands of similarity assessments per second.

6.2.2. Ensemble Maintenance
The LightGBM meta-classifier requires periodic retraining on new data to main-
tain optimal performance, introducing maintenance overhead and requiring labeled
training data collection. The extended feature set requires careful preprocessing and
normalization, increasing system complexity and creating additional potential points
of failure in production deployments.

6.2.3. Eviction Policy Constraints
While our Semantic Decay policy outperforms traditional approaches, it exhibits
parameter sensitivity. These weights may require domain-specific adjustment, and

                                           40
suboptimal tuning can significantly degrade cache performance. Additionally, seman-
tic similarity calculations for eviction decisions add computational cost compared to
simple LRU/LFU policies.


7. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a stacking ensemble for semantic caching in LLM-based sys-
tems, combining bi-encoder embeddings, cross-encoder scores, and a set of geometric
and text-based features through a trained LightGBM meta-classifier. To measure
how this approach performs in a deployment-relevant setting, we evaluate latency
and feature-group contributions on a held-out QQP subset, using the same dataset
and labeling scheme throughout. A feature-level ablation on this data shows that
most of the ensemble’s discriminative power comes from geometric features com-
puted directly from the bi-encoder embeddings, distance metrics and element-wise
operations, rather than from the cross-encoder score. Removing the cross-encoder
from the full model changes its F1 score by less than 0.001, while the cross-encoder
alone is responsible for over 95% of the pipeline’s added latency compared to the
bi-encoder (Section 5.3.3). This is a useful finding for anyone deploying a semantic
cache: the cross-encoder can be treated as an optional component, worth including
only when the last small gain in accuracy is more important than the roughly 500
ms of added latency per query that it introduces.

We evaluate the same ensemble on QQP and the STS benchmark, both of which carry
independently human-annotated labels, as our primary estimate of absolute caching
accuracy. On QQP, the ensemble reaches hit ratios of 93.3% for duplicate queries and
miss ratios of 80.7% for non-duplicate queries, and outperforms the best individual
bi-encoder and cross-encoder baselines by 4.2% and 5.2% in F1 score respectively


                                         41
(F1=0.8716±0.0040, AUC=0.9336±0.0049). Cross-dataset evaluation on the STS
benchmark shows a similar pattern, with F1 scores exceeding 0.867 and a Pearson
correlation of 0.8557 on both binary and regression formulations. Beyond similarity
detection, our results also show a 2.7× reduction in average response time, from 0.854
seconds to 0.311 seconds, and over 90% token savings on successfully cached queries,
confirming that the accuracy gains from the ensemble translate into real efficiency
gains at the system level.

It is important to note that traditional semantic caches, which store queries and
responses on a central server, pose significant privacy risks. Server-side caches con-
solidate queries from all users into a central database. Consequently, every query
submitted, whether it includes personal, professional, or sensitive information, is
stored and processed on a server outside the user’s control. Full anonymization of
text data is difficult to guarantee; even with explicit identifiers removed, a detailed
log of a user’s queries can often be linked back to them or reveal sensitive patterns
about their interests, work, or personal life. This tension between caching and pri-
vacy does not need to be unavoidable. Moving away from a centralized caching
model, toward federated learning for collaborative model improvement, user-local
semantic caches that avoid centralizing data, and fair resource-sharing algorithms on
the server side, makes it possible to build a semantic caching system that is both
efficient and respectful of user privacy (Kim et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a).

Looking ahead, several directions remain open. Our exploratory comparison of the
Semantic Decay eviction policy against LRU and LFU is limited in two ways that we
plan to address: the weights combining freshness, age, and semantic clustering were
tuned on a single workload without a sensitivity analysis, and the comparison does
not yet include other semantic-aware caching strategies from the literature, only non-

                                           42
semantic baselines. Closing both gaps, through a systematic weight sensitivity study
and a comparison against semantic-aware baselines such as adaptive thresholding, is
necessary before the eviction policy can be considered a validated contribution rather
than a preliminary result. Beyond this, we plan to extend our caching evaluation to
larger cache sizes closer to production scale, examine memory and scaling behavior as
the number of cached entries grows into the tens of thousands, and explore privacy-
preserving deployment of the caching system through the federated and user-local
approaches discussed above.
